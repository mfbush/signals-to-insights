# Final project

The six-standard calibration from session 1 is a project in miniature. It has a question (what is the unknown's concentration), data (six standards and one unknown), a method chosen for a property of the data (a line through the five standards inside the linear range) and a result with its reason (12.43 µM, not 12.68 µM, because the 25 µM standard sits where the response flattens). The final project does the same on a real dataset, over ten weeks, with a question you choose. This page sets out what you submit and when, how each part is scored, and the questions asked in the oral check-in.

## Your project repository

Your project lives in a GitHub repository of your own, from the topic declaration to the final submission, and that repository is what you submit. You make it from the course template, [signals-to-insights-project-template](https://github.com/mfbush/signals-to-insights-project-template), whose README gives every click.

1. Make a free GitHub account if you do not have one.
2. On the template's page, click **Use this template**, not **Fork**. Make your repository public, or private with the three of us added as collaborators: `mfbush` (Matt), `lnarisawa` (Lucas), and `ChrisJWeir` (Chris).
3. Clone it into your home folder beside your course folder, not inside it. Claude Code reads your repository's `AGENTS.md` only when no `CLAUDE.md` sits in the same folder or a folder above it, and the course folder has one.
4. Work through the template's three tasks in `tasks/`. Task 001 writes the `.gitignore` and task 002 copies the course environment, both due Sunday, November 1. Task 003 is the topic declaration.

Four files in the repository record how you worked. The final's Workflow criterion reads the first three, and its Judgment log criterion the fourth.

- `AGENTS.md`, in the top folder, holds the instructions Claude Code reads at the start of every conversation. The template's lines restate the course's rules, and you add lines about your own data.
- `.claude/skills/<name>/SKILL.md` holds one skill for a step you repeat, as in session 12. Unlike the course repository, your project repository commits it.
- `tasks/` holds one file per piece of work, each with dated progress entries, moved to `tasks/archive/` when it is done.
- `judgment-log.md`, in the top folder, is the judgment log described below.

Commit your data if they are under about 50 MB. Larger data stay out of the repository, listed in `.gitignore`, and a cell in your notebook downloads them from the source URL. GitHub refuses any file over 100 MB.

Each submitted milestone is the URL of one commit. A commit is a saved state of the whole repository, and its hash, a string of 40 letters and digits, names it. Push the commit first. Then open your repository on GitHub, click **Commits**, click the message of the commit you want read, and copy the address from your browser, which ends in `/commit/` and the hash. Submit that URL on Canvas. Your mentor clones your repository and reads it at that commit, so a commit pushed after your Canvas entry is not read. The time of the Canvas entry is the submission time for the deadline and the late rule.

## How big a project is

A finished project takes the first analysis from your working draft, adds one more method from the course, and makes one named comparison or check: an alternative method on the same data, a literature value, or a hand calculation. That check is what the "for a 5" items of the final's Method and Result criteria reward. The notebook runs to about 15 to 25 cells, and the project takes about 25 hours over the quarter.

A finished project does not need, and is not scored higher for, a second dataset, a method the course did not teach, or a written paper. The difference between a 4 and a 5 is one specific addition. On the final's Method criterion, for example, a 4 states the method, the property of the data that chose it, and an assumption checked with its result. A 5 adds a named alternative run on the same data with a number, such as the residual standard deviation of a linear and a quadratic calibration on your standards.

An example repository made from the template, on course data, will be linked here by Tuesday, November 3.

## How the written parts are scored

The working draft and the final submission are scored on criteria worth 0 to 5 each. Each criterion lists its elements, and one scale applies to all of them. "Grounded" means what it means for the weekly judgment cell: it cites values or named features from your own output that match your notebook, and connects them to the chemical or statistical reason.

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 5 | Every element is there and grounded, and the criterion's "for a 5" item is met |
| 4 | Every element is there and grounded |
| 3 | Every element is there, but at least one is the right idea with nothing specific to your data |
| 2 | One element is missing, or most are the right idea only |
| 1 | The elements appear in name only: a figure with no description, a method with no reason, a number with no unit |
| 0 | Missing |

Length and polish are not criteria. A reason in one sentence that cites your residuals outscores a page that could describe anyone's data.

## The judgment log

The judgment log is a Markdown file, `judgment-log.md`, in your project folder. It holds judgment entries in the same format as part (b) of the weekly judgment cell, each about your project's data; entries from the weekly activities do not count toward it. It needs at least two entries by the working draft and at least four by the final submission, two of them written after November 22. An entry about a suggestion you verified counts when it names the check and its result.

For the final submission the log opens with a paragraph of 100 to 200 words on how you used AI in the project: the tasks you used it for, the tasks you did not, and what you checked and how. You write the log and the paragraph yourself, under the same rule as the judgment cell on the [AI practices page](ai-practices.md).

```markdown
# Judgment log: <your name>

## How I used AI in this project

(Final submission only.)

## Entries

### Nov 12: baseline for the 1650 to 1750 cm⁻¹ region

- **What the assistant suggested:** ...
- **What I did:** accepted, changed, rejected or verified it.
- **Why, from my data:** ...
```

## 3. Oral check-in

Session 16, on Tuesday, November 24, has no opening: the check-ins start at 1:30 pm and run through the session, including its office hour, in the order posted on Canvas; the rest of the session is project work, and Matt sits in on some check-ins. You spend seven minutes with your mentor, your notebook and log open on your laptop. There are no slides, and nothing to prepare except your own reasoning. Your mentor asks three or four questions from the bank below, chosen from your notebook, and follows up on your answers.

The check-in is scored on four dimensions at 0 to 5 each, 0 to 20 in all: Data, Method, Result and Judgment, matching the four groups of questions. A 5 needs an answer to a follow-up question you could not have prepared for.

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 5 | Grounded in your notebook, and you answer the follow-up ("what happens to the slope if that point goes?") from your own data |
| 4 | Grounded; the follow-up gets a general answer |
| 3 | The right idea, correct, with nothing specific to your data |
| 2 | You say what was done but not why |
| 1 | Vague or partly wrong, or the reason given is that the assistant suggested it |
| 0 | No answer, or an answer your notebook contradicts |

A total of 9 or below earns a second check-in in session 19, on Tuesday, December 8, with Matt. The higher of the two scores counts, up to 14. If you cannot attend session 16, tell your mentor before 1:30 pm that day and you are checked in during session 19 with no cap. A check-in missed without notice scores 0, unless you give a documented reason within 24 hours.

### Question bank

The bracketed parts are filled in from your notebook.

**Data**

1. Where did your data come from, and what does one row represent experimentally?
2. What is the unit of your main measured variable, and how did you check that it loaded correctly?
3. What problem did you find when you first loaded the data, and what did you do about it?
4. Is your dataset representative of the system your question is about? What would make it not representative?

**Method**

5. Why did you choose [your method] over [an alternative from the course]? What property of your data decides it?
6. What does [your method] assume about your data, and how did you check that assumption?
7. If the method had failed (the fit did not converge, or the model did not generalize), what would you have done instead?
8. With ten times as much data, would you choose a different method?
9. What did you decide not to do, and why?

**Result**

10. Walk me through your main result so far. What does it say about your question?
11. What is the uncertainty on that result, and where in your workflow does it come from?
12. Your notebook shows [a trend, an outlier, an unexpected value]. What is the chemical explanation?
13. Which part of your analysis would you least want to defend to a skeptical reviewer?

**Judgment**

14. Walk me through [an entry in your log]: what the assistant got wrong, or what you checked, and how you caught it.
15. Where in your notebook did you accept the assistant's code unchanged? How do you know it is right for your data?
16. Would you make the same decision in [an entry in your log] again, now that you are further into the analysis?
17. What question about your project did you ask the assistant and find you could not trust its answer to? How did you settle it?

## 4. Final submission

Submit the URL of a pushed commit that holds the notebook, the data or a cell that downloads them, `judgment-log.md`, the visual summary as a PDF, `AGENTS.md`, your skill, and `tasks/`. There is no zip file.

The notebook runs from a clean start in the course environment, which your repository's copies of `pyproject.toml`, `uv.lock` and `.python-version` rebuild. Its first markdown cell states the question, the data source with URL and access date, and a data and code availability statement; its last markdown cell states the result with its uncertainty and what it means for the question. Any random step uses a fixed seed. Anything in the course `pyproject.toml` may be used.

The visual summary is one page, letter size or a single 16:9 slide. Its first line is your question and its second line is your main finding with its uncertainty. It carries at most three figures, each with a one sentence description beneath it, in a colorblind-safe palette, and it cites the data source.

The final submission is scored on seven criteria, 0 to 35 in all.

| Criterion | Elements | For a 5 |
|---|---|---|
| Reproducibility | Runs from a clean start with relative paths; data included or downloaded by a cell; seeds fixed; availability statement in the first cell | States what a reader would need that the notebook cannot supply |
| Question and data | The question in one sentence; source with URL and access date; units and what a row represents; what was removed and why | States what these data cannot answer |
| Method | The method; the property of the data that chose it; an assumption checked, with the result of the check | Compares it with a named alternative on your data, with a number |
| Result | The main result with units and uncertainty; interpreted against the chemistry (magnitude, expected range); limits stated | Checks the result against an independent value: a literature value, a second method or a hand calculation |
| Visual summary | Question and finding in the first two lines; at most three figures, each with a description; source cited; one page | A reader can state your finding and its uncertainty from the page alone |
| Judgment log | At least four entries, two written after November 22, each grounded; the paragraph on how you used AI | One entry is about a decision that changed your result, with the value before and after |
| Workflow | `AGENTS.md` steers the assistant on this project's data; one skill at `.claude/skills/<name>/SKILL.md` reproduces a repeated step and was used, with its output in the notebook; `tasks/` records the work in dated entries and closed tasks; each grounded | One of the three changed a number or a decision, with the value before and after |
